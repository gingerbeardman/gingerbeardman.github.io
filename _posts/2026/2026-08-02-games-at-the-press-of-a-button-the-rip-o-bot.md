[Fukio Mitsuji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fukio_Mitsuji) (三辻富貴朗)—known by his nickname "MTJ", squeezed from the consonants of his surname, *Mi-Tsu-Ji*—was the game designer behind *Bubble Bobble*, *Rainbow Islands*, *Syvalion*, *Volfied*, and *Magical Puzzle Popils*. Surely one of the greatest to walk these hallowed halls.

In the late 1980s he also wrote a column for the Japanese arcade magazine *GAMEST* called ゲームデザイン入門—*Introduction to Game Design*—aimed at readers who dreamed of becoming game designers themselves.

![IMG](/images/posts/mtj-gamest-31-prediction.jpg)

In part 2 of the series, published in [*GAMEST* No. 31 (April 1989)](https://archive.org/details/gamest0031/page/n77/mode/2up), MTJ is railing against imitation games—パクリ, "rip-offs"—and the industry attitude that produces them. He quotes a manager at an unnamed company:

> 「10年先!? その頃には今のゲームを知らない世代がプレイヤー層をしめてるさ！ だからあえて変える必要もない！ あとは目さき（外見）さえ変えてやりゃあ…プレイヤーなんて与えられたものをやるだけさ!!」
>
> *"Ten years from now!? By then the player base will be a generation that doesn't even know today's games! So there's no need to go changing anything! Just tweak the surface—the outside—and… players just play whatever they're handed!!"*

Then, to show where that road leads, he writes a little scene of science fiction. Set in "the very near future" (超近未来), at the development office of the fictional Dekoboko Co., a game is created by conversing with a machine:

> 「ゴシュジンサマ、コンドハナンノゲームノパクリデイキヤショウカ？ インカムバッチリデッセ!?」
>
> *"MASTER, WHICH GAME SHALL WE RIP OFF THIS TIME? THE TAKINGS'LL BE SPLENDID!?"*
>
> 「ゲームメイヲ、ニュウリョクシナハレ！ ハヨ、センカイ！ ワレ！」
>
> *"ENTER THE GAME NAME, IF YOU'D BE SO KIND—COME ON, GET ON WITH IT, YOU!"*
>
> ○✕社の△□と入力！ いたって簡単だ。
>
> *I punch in Company ○×'s "△□". Dead simple.*
>
> 「OK! OK! ツギハハイケイノイメージデゴワス。ツギノナカカラエランデタモレ」
>
> *"OK! OK! NEXT IS THE BACKGROUND IMAGE, SIR. PRAY CHOOSE FROM THE FOLLOWING."*
>
> 私は億というファイルからそれを見つけるのがめんどうなので、いつもの様にランダムチョイスのキーを押した！ 1発でOKだ！
>
> *Digging that out of hundreds of millions of files is a pain, so as usual I hit the random-choice key! One shot and it's done!*
>
> 「サテ、ツギハキャラクタージャケン、…ツギノナカカラ…」
>
> *"RIGHT THEN, NEXT IT'S THE CHARACTERS, SO IT IS… FROM THE FOLLOWING…"*
>
> 「えぇーい!!」
>
> *"Aaaargh!!"*
>
> 私はいいかげんうんざりしたので、全て、ランダムチョイスのキーを押しまくった!!
>
> *I'd had quite enough, so I just hammered the random-choice key for everything!!*
>
> 「出来た!! 大傑作だ。大傑作の…コピーゲームだ。これで我が社も丸もうけ」
>
> *"IT'S DONE!! A MASTERPIECE. A MASTERPIECE OF A… COPY GAME. Our company's going to clean up with this one."*

A game, generated in moments, from a prompt. In 1989.

(In the original, the robot speaks entirely in katakana, its speech wobbling between Kansai, Kyushu, and Hiroshima dialects with the odd flourish of archaic courtly Japanese—エランデタモレ, "pray choose". English can't really reproduce that, so here it's an over-formal servant whose register keeps slipping.)

MTJ even gives his machine a name: the パクリングロボット (*Pakuringu Robotto*). パクリング is a word you won't find in any dictionary—it's his own coinage, the slang パクリ (*pakuri*, "rip-off") bolted onto an English *-ing*, as in サンプリング (sampling) or ボウリング (bowling). A jokey pseudo-word built from real parts, which is why I can't resist translating it as the *Rip-O-Bot*. He follows the skit with a warning that lands rather differently reading it today:

> と言ったかどうかは知らないけど、こんな時代がもうそこまで来ているのは事実です。
>
> *Whether it happens exactly like that or not I don't know, but it's a fact that an era like this is very nearly upon us.*
>
> 199X年、平凡なゲームデザイナー達は、「パクリングロボット」の出現により、大量に解雇！ てな事になった時、君にはこんなロボット連中に負けないだけの意気込みとオリジナリティーがはたして残ってるだろうか？
>
> *199X. When ordinary game designers are laid off en masse thanks to the arrival of the "Rip-O-Bot"—when that happens, will you still have enough drive and originality left in you to beat robots like those?*
>
> 物真ねならロボットにだって出来る！ とは思いませんか？ 人間らしい独創的な事に頭と心を使おうではありませんか!!
>
> *Robots can do imitation too!—don't you agree? Let's put our heads and our hearts into the sort of original work that only humans can do.*

He was out by a couple of decades on the date, but the shape of the thing is exactly right: a machine that can produce a passable derivative work at the press of a button, and the question of what that leaves for the people who used to make them. His answer is the thesis of the whole column, emphasised in the original:

> 「ゲームは行きつくところアイデアだ！」
>
> *"In the end, a game IS the idea!"*

Elsewhere on the page he makes the same point from the other direction: graphics will eventually have "a reality you can't tell apart from the real thing", so judging a game by its looks is a dead end; and the era where "anyone can make a game" had, in his view, already arrived—learn a few basic techniques of game feel and presentation and even "the ladies next door" (隣のおばさん達) could turn out a passable imitation. If the only difference between you and them is technique, you have no difference at all. The idea, he says, is "the one and only, final *weapon* left to you" (あなたに残された唯一、最後の"武器").

## The Osamu Tezuka of the game world

There's a poignant footnote to the page. Osamu Tezuka, the "god of manga", had died on 9 February 1989, shortly before this issue went to press. The page carries a hand-drawn cartoon of a young designer-to-be, pencil in pocket, gazing up at a thought bubble containing Tezuka in his trademark beret:

> ほんとうに偉大なまんが家 手塚治虫氏がなくなった…
>
> *The truly great manga artist Osamu Tezuka has passed away…*
>
> はたして ゲーム界の手塚治虫は 出現するのだろうか？…
>
> *Will the Osamu Tezuka of the game world ever appear?…*

Which is, really, the same question as the Rip-O-Bot's, asked in the opposite direction. The machine sets the floor: imitation is free. A Tezuka sets the ceiling: the original work that only a human—head and heart—can do.

MTJ died in 2008, at the age of 48, long before the arrival of the Rip-O-Bots. But he'd already written the response, thirty-something years early: robots can do imitation too. So don't compete with them there.

----

## Fuel for Jinks

This page is one of the things that fuelled the creation of [Jinks](/2026/07/30/hi-jinks/), my new way to make games. Because MTJ's question is now *the* question: if a game can be made at the click of a button, how do we more easily make novel, unique, or better games in this new world?

My answer is the same as his. Move the focus towards the ideas, and towards a better way to represent them: the gameplay, the systems, the mechanics, the rules, the scoring. Those are the parts of a game that are actually fun to work on—and the parts that make it *yours*—so that's where your time should go, rather than into reinventing the wheel and reimplementing the same boilerplate code over and over.

The Rip-O-Bots can make copycat games. The idea is still the weapon.
